Protecting federal information and systems

What Congress required, what OMB directed, and how NIST turned those duties into standards and guidance. A source-led history from 2000 to 2026, with the earlier foundations.

Research checked 25 October 2026 · 41 milestones · 80 direct quotations

Two objects of protection. Duties that overlap.

The law tells agencies to protect their information and their systems. It does not make ownership of equipment the only test. Later policy explains what agencies must do when another organization handles the information or provides the system.

Three points prevent common misreadings:

  1. Risk assessment and compliance go together. FISMA requires both. A duty to understand risk is not an alternative to applicable standards and policy.
  2. “Outside our boundary” does not mean “outside our responsibility.” NIST distinguishes external services from the system that uses them while preserving the agency’s information and risk duties.
  3. The nonfederal-system path has conditions. The CUI rule and SP 800-171 provide an explicit, narrower requirements set. They do not cover every external service or every kind of federal information.

Before 2000The information duty came first

PRA: give OMB responsibility for information policy

1980Law

The Paperwork Reduction Act gave OMB an information-management role that included security. The quoted duty belongs to OMB’s Director. Its object is information collected or maintained by or on behalf of agencies.

Where it came from
Congress assigned the policy function in the PRA. OMB later identified the PRA as the authority behind A-130.
What changed—or stayed
This is an early information-based duty. It does not depend on the government owning the equipment that holds the information.

The Computer Security Act includes processing services

1988Law

The Computer Security Act of 1987 was enacted on January 8, 1988. Its definition joined system operation to processing information for a federal function. It also assigned standards work to the National Bureau of Standards, later renamed NIST.

Where it came from
Congress directed the Bureau to develop standards and send them to Commerce for promulgation. The Act also required agency security plans and training (§§5–6).
What changed—or stayed
Private operation was already within the statutory definition in specified circumstances. “On behalf of” did not start with cloud computing or FISMA.

A-130 explains its PRA roots—and the role of agreements

1993OMB policy

OMB’s 1993 revision focused on information management, dissemination, and records. Its explanatory appendix cautioned that agencies’ information responsibilities were not automatically identical to those of grantees and contractors.

Where it came from
The notice expressly says OMB issued the original 1985 circular to fulfill its PRA responsibilities. The second quotation is OMB’s analysis of “Government Information,” not a later FISMA exemption.
What changed—or stayed
Agency duties could be carried into grants and contracts. Read this historically: later security statutes and policies added requirements, and the 2016 circular replaced this edition.

PRA: agencies must protect information “by or for” them

1995Law

The 1995 PRA restated agency responsibilities. Section 3506(g) combined compliance, accountability, and protection proportionate to risk. It expressly referred to the Computer Security Act.

Where it came from
This is Congress’s agency-level duty, separate from the Director’s policy role quoted above.
What changed—or stayed
Notice the different phrases: paragraph (1) says “by or for the agency”; paragraph (3) says “by or on behalf of an agency.” Both concern information. Later amendments changed the cross-references; this is the 1995 enactment.

A-130 requires risk-based protection, not one method of analysis

1996OMB policy

A-130 §8(a)(1)(g) directed agencies to protect government information in proportion to risk. The revised security appendix also addressed system plans, controls, reviews, and authorization.

Where it came from
In its response to public comments, OMB linked risk-based controls to the Computer Security Act. Some commenters opposed removing formal risk analyses; others found them too costly.
What changed—or stayed
OMB kept risk assessment essential while declining to require a formal risk analysis for every system. Risk-based judgment concerned how to meet the duty; it did not remove the duty.

2000–2004Two duties, then a standards program

GISRA makes security an agency-wide program

2000Law

The Government Information Security Reform provisions required protections for information and systems supporting agency operations. They also required an agency-wide program, periodic assessments, testing, and independent evaluation.

Where it came from
Congress enacted these provisions in the FY2001 defense authorization law, Public Law 106–398. Former 44 USC 3534 supplied the agency duties; §3535 supplied independent evaluations.
What changed—or stayed
The law quoted here uses “information collected or maintained by or for the agency.” It predates the two separately numbered objects of protection in FISMA 2002. The temporary GISRA framework was followed by FISMA.

The 2000 A-130 revision chiefly implements Clinger–Cohen

2000OMB policy

The 2000 revision addressed IT investment and management. Its existing security appendix also explained how agencies should decide whether to interconnect systems.

Where it came from
The final notice expressly identifies Clinger–Cohen as the reason for this revision. Its timing near GISRA does not establish that GISRA caused the revision.
What changed—or stayed
Information moving between systems already called for safeguards and a management decision about risk. This is a connection decision, not a statement that every connected system becomes an agency system.

FISMA writes the two duties side by side

2002Law

The December 2002 Federal Information Security Management Act required agency heads to protect two things: information collected or maintained by or on behalf of the agency, and systems used or operated by the agency or for it. The same risk standard governs both.

Where it came from
Title III of the E-Government Act, Public Law 107–347, enacted former 44 USC 3544. November’s Homeland Security Act also contained FISMA provisions; this report quotes the December enactment that supplied this framework.
What changed—or stayed
The agency-wide program expressly includes information and systems provided or managed by another agency, a contractor, or another source. The statute does not say “only the components the agency directly controls.” Nor does it define every external service as a federal system.

Congress sends work directly to NIST and Commerce

2002Law

FISMA did more than tell OMB to issue policy. It directly assigned NIST standards and guideline work and set deadlines for categorization standards, mapping guidance, and minimum requirements.

Where it came from
FISMA §303 amended the NIST Act. Section 302 amended 40 USC 11331 on Commerce’s prescription of standards. NIST develops; Commerce approves binding standards under the statutory process.
What changed—or stayed
This is why the history is not simply law → OMB → NIST. There is also a direct law → NIST/Commerce path. FIPS 199, SP 800-60, and FIPS 200 identify themselves as responses to the assigned tasks.

OMB explains incidental equipment and NIST’s role

2003–2004OMB policy

OMB’s first FISMA reporting instructions already said an agency remained responsible for federal information used on incidentally acquired equipment. In 2004, OMB also distinguished mandatory FIPS from NIST guidance.

Where it came from
M-03-19 and M-04-25 implemented FISMA reporting and interpretation. They referred agencies to A-130 Appendix III for appropriate controls.
What changed—or stayed
An equipment exclusion in procurement law did not erase information-security responsibility. The 2004 answer also cautions against treating every sentence in a NIST publication as if it had the same status as a FIPS requirement.

FIPS 199: categorize information and systems

2004Mandatory standard

FIPS 199 supplies low, moderate, and high impact categories for confidentiality, integrity, and availability. The impact of losing information helps determine the protection a system needs.

Where it came from
The standard explicitly identifies FISMA’s first task: categorization. Its applicability section distinguishes information from federal information systems and states the classified-information and national-security-system exceptions.
What changed—or stayed
Categorization is a required input to decisions. It does not itself choose every control or decide whether an external system operates on an agency’s behalf.

SP 800-37: evidence for an authorization decision

2004NIST guidance

The first SP 800-37 explained security certification and accreditation. Certification assessed controls; accreditation was a senior official’s decision to authorize operation and accept the resulting risk.

Where it came from
The publication cites its FISMA responsibilities and says it is consistent with A-130 §8(b)(3). It expressly attributes the accreditation requirement to A-130 Appendix III.
What changed—or stayed
This connects statutory responsibility and OMB policy to a practical system-level decision. Risk acceptance follows evidence about controls; it is not permission to disregard applicable requirements.

SP 800-60: map information types to impact categories

2004NIST guidance

The second FISMA task was guidance on which information and systems belong in each category. SP 800-60 provided a method and provisional impact levels for information types.

Where it came from
Its executive summary explicitly says it responds to the second task and assumes familiarity with FIPS 199.
What changed—or stayed
The information and the system are linked: the system’s use, connectivity, and aggregate information content inform its category. The mapping is a starting point to review, not an automatic answer for every agency.

2005–2009Controls, boundaries, and external services

SP 800-53: outsourced services have control obligations

2005NIST guidance

The first SP 800-53 supplied a catalog and baselines of controls. SA-9 addressed outsourced information-system services and required the organization to monitor compliance.

Where it came from
This was part of NIST’s FISMA standards-and-guidelines work. The following year, FIPS 200 expressly required control selection using SP 800-53.
What changed—or stayed
The original SA-9 guidance described the same control and documentation requirements as internal systems. That wording matters historically; later editions describe external services and trust in more detail.

FIPS 200: use the impact category to select controls

2006Mandatory standard

FIPS 200 established minimum security requirements. Agencies meet them through appropriately tailored SP 800-53 baselines, using the FIPS 199 categorization.

Where it came from
The standard’s announcement identifies FISMA as its authority and Commerce as the approving authority. Section 4 explicitly connects FIPS 199 to SP 800-53; footnote 5 calls for the current SP 800-53, as amended.
What changed—or stayed
Risk assessment and tailoring are part of compliance. They do not turn minimum requirements into optional advice. FIPS 200’s applicability covers federal information and federal systems, with stated exceptions; the standard does not declare every private system federal.

SP 800-18 Revision 1: define the system being planned for

2006NIST guidance

The system-security-plan guide tells agencies to categorize the system and its information before drawing boundaries and selecting controls. A boundary identifies the resources covered by the plan.

Where it came from
The revision connects system planning to FIPS 199 and the SP 800-53 baseline process.
What changed—or stayed
“Direct management control” is a guide for grouping resources. The text expressly allows intervening management. It is not a statutory test that excludes every resource an agency cannot administer directly.

SP 800-53 Revisions 1 and 2: outside the boundary still matters

2006–2007NIST guidance

SA-9 changed from “Outsourced” to “External Information System Services.” Its explanation covered services used by a system but outside its accreditation boundary, including several forms of business relationship.

Where it came from
These are revisions of the control catalog used through FIPS 200. The two quotations below are from Revision 1 and Revision 2 respectively.
What changed—or stayed
The quoted passages are identical in the collected editions. The important distinction is between the authorization boundary and responsibility for risks arising from an external service. Being outside the boundary does not end that responsibility.

SP 800-60 Revision 1 keeps information as the basis for categorization

2008NIST guidance

The revised mapping guide still starts with information types and their consequences. It retains the explicit link to FISMA’s second task and FIPS 199.

Where it came from
This updates the 2004 mapping guidance. The excerpt shows the retained method rather than a new rule for outsourcing.
What changed—or stayed
The reference to the system’s use, connectivity, and aggregate information content remains. This edition does not replace the separate legal inquiry about who uses or operates a system and for what purpose.

SP 800-53 Revision 3 makes oversight roles explicit

2009 / updated 2010NIST guidance

SA-9 now lists three duties: require compliant controls, document government oversight and user roles, and monitor the provider’s compliance.

Where it came from
This continues the external-services control in Revisions 1 and 2. The catalog supports the FIPS 200 control-selection process.
What changed—or stayed
The control text makes the oversight allocation explicit. The saved Revision 3 PDF incorporates an August 2010 update, so this quotation is evidence for that edition, not proof of the precise day each word first appeared.

Edition note. Revision 3 first published August 2009; saved file identifies August 2010 updates and includes an archive cover.

2010–2013Purchased services and continuing oversight

SP 800-37 Revision 1 extends the risk framework across the life cycle

2010NIST guidance

The revised guide organized categorization, control selection, implementation, assessment, authorization, and monitoring into the Risk Management Framework. Its discussion of complex systems expressly addresses external subsystems.

Where it came from
The passage cites SP 800-53 Appendix I, “Security Controls in External Environments,” and attributes its requirement to FISMA and OMB policy.
What changed—or stayed
NIST applies federal security requirements to external subsystems handling federal information and their associated services. Its surrounding discussion acknowledges limited direct control and the need for evidence and trust. This wording predates the 2016 A-130 revision.

OMB expressly includes subscribed and outsourced services

2010OMB policy

The FY2010 instructions connect FISMA’s agency-program requirement to fully or partly provided services, expressly including software as a service. They separately retain agency accountability for information on incidental equipment.

Where it came from
M-10-15 questions 36–38 interpret the existing FISMA framework and refer back to A-130 and NIST. This extends the earlier reporting guidance into more explicit service language.
What changed—or stayed
The historical instruction to use “identical, not equivalent” procedures is particularly strong. It belongs to these 2010 instructions. Later FedRAMP policy provides reuse and authorization paths; do not treat this sentence alone as the complete current process.

Records obligations also follow information into a service

2010Guidance

NARA explained that records-management responsibilities need to be addressed in cloud contracts. Information security does not replace recordkeeping, retention, access, or lawful disposal.

Where it came from
NARA Bulletin 2010-05 applies records-management responsibilities to cloud environments. This is a related information-management stream, not the origin of FISMA or the RMF.
What changed—or stayed
An agency can retain records responsibilities even when another organization supplies the service. The security authorization alone does not settle those duties.

FedRAMP provides a common assessment and authorization process

2011OMB policy

OMB’s December memorandum established FedRAMP policy for agencies procuring cloud services. Its scope expressly included commercial and non-commercial services, public and other deployment models, and the principal service models.

Where it came from
The memorandum built a reusable approach around existing FISMA and NIST requirements. NIST SP 800-144 separately explained why security and privacy accountability remains with the agency.
What changed—or stayed
A shared service and an external operator did not, by themselves, remove the service from the policy. The memorandum contained a specific on-premises, agency-only private-cloud exception; that exception still required FISMA and NIST compliance.

SP 800-53 Revision 4 ties external services to federal requirements

2013 / updated 2015NIST guidance

SA-9 again distinguishes the service from the organizational system’s authorization boundary. It expressly covers providers processing, storing, or transmitting federal information, or operating systems for the government.

Where it came from
The guidance states its connection to FISMA and OMB policy. Revision 4 also brought privacy controls into the catalog.
What changed—or stayed
The requirement is not limited to systems inside an agency-drawn boundary. The organization must establish what the external service protects and how compliance will be monitored. This is guidance about applying duties, not a new act of Congress.

Edition note. Revision 4 published April 2013; this saved PDF includes updates through January 22, 2015.

OMB requires a strategy for continuing authorization and monitoring

2013OMB policy

M-14-03 directed agencies to implement continuous monitoring of controls in phases through FY2017. The strategy had to address ongoing authorizations and environments of operation.

Where it came from
The memorandum explicitly cites the RMF and NIST guidance, including SP 800-37, 800-39, 800-53, 800-53A, and 800-137.
What changed—or stayed
The management task continues after initial approval. Agencies need a way to learn about changing risks and control performance, including where they rely on shared controls.

2014–2016The statutory continuity and the CUI split

The RMF update retains its external-services wording

June 2014NIST guidance

The June update to SP 800-37 Revision 1 retained the passage about external providers, subsystems, and associated services.

Where it came from
The original February 2010 text and the June 2014 updated edition are preserved separately. Their quoted passages match.
What changed—or stayed
This update preceded the December 2014 FISMA law. It cannot be described as implementing that later enactment.

FISMA modernization retains the two objects of protection

December 2014Law

The Federal Information Security Modernization Act of 2014 retained the information/system distinction in 44 USC 3554. Compare the first quotation with 2002: the shared duty and both objects have the same wording.

Where it came from
Public Law 113–283 replaced the prior subchapter and revised federal oversight and operational responsibilities, including OMB and DHS roles. The quoted agency provisions are §§3554(a) and (b).
What changed—or stayed
Agencies must assess harm, determine appropriate security, implement risk-reducing policies, and test controls. The agency-wide program still includes externally provided or managed information and systems. Risk management and compliance are concurrent duties.

SP 800-171 creates a tailored path for CUI in nonfederal systems

2015NIST guidance

SP 800-171 addressed the confidentiality of controlled unclassified information held in qualifying nonfederal systems. CUI is information requiring safeguards under a law, regulation, or government-wide policy; it is not a synonym for all federal information.

Where it came from
The publication was developed for the CUI program established by Executive Order 13556. Its requirements were derived from federal standards and SP 800-53, tailored for this narrower setting. The 2015 text referred to a proposed CUI rule.
What changed—or stayed
The distinction was already express: the main scope excluded systems used or operated for agencies, while footnote 8 also excluded organizations collecting or maintaining information for them. Read the footnote with the main text. SP 800-171 was never a general substitute for federal-system requirements.

Edition note. June 2015 publication; saved PDF incorporates January 2016 updates. Do not read its references to a proposed rule as final 2016 rule text.

A-130 separates definitions and spells out agency oversight

July 2016OMB policy

The revised circular defines federal information with lifecycle verbs and federal information systems with use/operation language. Appendix I then addresses agreements and oversight of non-federal entities.

Where it came from
Section 9 cites the PRA, E-Government Act, FISMA 2014, Clinger–Cohen, and other authorities. Appendix I §5(r) expressly explains FISMA applicability; §4(j) gives the operative agency directions.
What changed—or stayed
Section 4(j)(1) covers agreements involving the lifecycle of federal information. Section 4(j)(2) has an “or”: systems used or operated for government, OR systems collecting or maintaining federal information for it. Its oversight duties include controls, inventory, interfaces, access, and incident response. An information-handling arrangement can therefore trigger more than a general instruction to understand risk.

The CUI rule states an explicit federal/nonfederal split

September–November 2016Regulation

NARA’s final rule distinguishes systems operated for an agency from other nonfederal systems holding CUI. The first category includes outsourced processing services, even when the system serves both government and private users.

Where it came from
The rule implements Executive Order 13556 and incorporates FIPS 199, FIPS 200, SP 800-53, and SP 800-171. It was published September 14 and effective November 14, 2016.
What changed—or stayed
For qualifying nonfederal systems, the rule prohibits treating them as agency systems and directs agencies to use SP 800-171 for CUI confidentiality, with stated exceptions. Its incidental-handling language expressly excludes processing services from that example. This is a scoped CUI rule, not a blanket exemption for commercial systems.

SP 800-171 Revision 1 makes both exclusions explicit in the main scope

December 2016 / updated 2018NIST guidance

Revision 1’s scope says both “not collecting or maintaining information” for an agency and not using or operating a system for one. The earlier edition put the information condition in a footnote; the revised main paragraph makes it visible.

Where it came from
The revision responds to the now-final CUI framework and continues the FIPS/SP 800-53 tailoring approach.
What changed—or stayed
Compared with the collected 2015 edition, Revision 1 also includes requirement 3.12.4 on system security plans. A nonfederal system can have a documented plan and connections without becoming an agency system. The applicable authority and agreement determine the obligation.

Edition note. The quoted Revision 1 file includes updates through June 7, 2018. Intermediate 2017/2018 update wording is not separately reconstructed here.

2018–2021Information owners, external systems, and exchanges

SP 800-37 Revision 2 distinguishes information stewardship from system ownership

2018NIST guidance

The revised RMF integrates security and privacy and adds organizational preparation. Its role descriptions make a useful distinction: the information owner retains protection responsibilities when sharing information, while the authorizing official can authorize use of an external service.

Where it came from
The publication cites FISMA 2014 and the 2016 A-130. Appendix G continues the external-provider discussion from the earlier RMF and control catalog.
What changed—or stayed
NIST pairs a strong statement about applicable requirements with practical risk responses where direct control is limited. “Authorization to use” and an acceptable exchange risk do not make statutory or policy requirements disappear. They describe how the agency decides whether to rely on the service.

SP 800-171 Revision 2 explains agreements and bounded protection

2020 / updated 2021NIST guidance

The front matter states when these recommended requirements apply to a nonfederal organization: when an agency mandates them in a contract, grant, or other agreement. The scope remains CUI confidentiality in qualifying nonfederal systems.

Where it came from
Revision 2 continues the CUI rule and SP 800-53-based approach. Its §1.1 retains the information-handling and system-operation exclusions.
What changed—or stayed
The publication expressly permits a separate CUI security domain to limit the components that must satisfy these requirements. That is a concrete scoping provision. It is not a rule limiting all FISMA duties to an agency-controlled tenant.

Edition note. February 2020 edition, including January 28, 2021 updates; later withdrawal cover is retained.

SP 800-53 Revision 5: controls for organizations and services

2020NIST guidance

Revision 5’s SA-9 requires external providers to meet organizational security and privacy requirements, documents oversight roles, and calls for ongoing monitoring. Its discussion expressly contemplates that the organization has no direct control over implementation or assessment.

Where it came from
Revision 5 is the successor control catalog. Baselines moved to the companion SP 800-53B; FIPS 200 continues to connect federal minimum requirements to control selection.
What changed—or stayed
The catalog supports federal and nonfederal uses. A control’s presence in it is not, by itself, proof that every private company must implement it. Selection, applicable law and policy, and agreements supply that link.

Edition note. September 2020 publication, including December 10, 2020 updates. Later release changes are addressed below.

SP 800-47 Revision 1 centers the information exchange

2021NIST guidance

The revised guide addresses information before, during, and after an exchange. It is technology-neutral and applies across different organizations or different authorization boundaries within one organization.

Where it came from
It expands the earlier interconnection-security focus into management of information exchanges. Its guidance complements the RMF and A-130’s interface and agreement duties.
What changed—or stayed
Protecting exchanged information and authorizing the systems are related but distinct work. Participating organizations need to identify risks, required protections, responsible officials, and suitable agreements. Connectivity alone does not decide whether one organization operates for another.

2022–2026Cloud legislation and current guidance

Congress codifies FedRAMP and directs OMB to define its scope

2022Law

The FedRAMP Authorization Act establishes a reusable assessment and authorization program for cloud products and services processing unclassified information used by agencies. It imports A-130’s definitions of federal information and authorization to operate.

Where it came from
Public Law 117–263 §5921 adds 44 USC 3607–3616. Section 3614 specifically directs OMB to issue scope and authorization guidance.
What changed—or stayed
The statute’s reference to services operated as federal information systems must be read with OMB’s ensuing scope policy. Reusing a FedRAMP package also does not remove an agency’s FISMA responsibility: §3613(e)(2) preserves it expressly.

DHS declines to adopt NARA’s definition for all of its FISMA rule

2023Comment response

Public comments on DHS’s safeguarding rule questioned how it distinguished federal and nonfederal systems and how it used “on behalf of an agency.” DHS intentionally declined to import the CUI rule’s definition.

Where it came from
This is DHS’s response in the final-rule preamble, 88 FR 40571. The agency argued that a general FISMA definition should come from OMB after interagency coordination.
What changed—or stayed
The response is evidence of an unresolved difference in agency interpretation. It does not repeal NARA’s CUI rule, supply a court holding, or establish a universal commercial-service exemption. Its immediate context is DHS acquisition rules.

SP 800-171 Revision 3 retains the narrower scope

May 2024NIST guidance

Revision 3 continues to exclude organizations collecting or maintaining information for an agency, or using or operating its systems. Its requirements apply to components handling CUI or protecting those components and are intended for agreements with agencies.

Where it came from
The revision updates the requirements using the SP 800-53 Revision 5 catalog. NIST’s February 2024 public-comment analysis discusses alignment with source controls and tailoring for CUI confidentiality.
What changed—or stayed
This is the current final SP 800-171 edition checked for this report. The component limit is express. The publication’s information-handling exclusion is equally express; neither should be omitted when deciding whether this is the right requirements set.

OMB defines FedRAMP scope using information-handling verbs

July 2024OMB policy

M-24-15 describes in-scope cloud products and services as those creating, collecting, processing, storing, or maintaining federal information for an agency, subject to the memorandum’s exclusions.

Where it came from
The memorandum implements the FedRAMP Authorization Act and replaces the 2011 cloud authorization memorandum. The statute directed OMB to specify the categories and characteristics in scope.
What changed—or stayed
FedRAMP scope is a program decision with express exclusions, not a universal definition for every law. Agencies use the package for their own authorization to operate or use; other information-management, privacy, records, and security duties remain.

SP 800-53 Release 5.2.0 updates controls without replacing the basic framework

2025NIST guidance

The August release targets software updates, patches, development, and resilience. Its official change list identifies the affected controls and assessment procedures.

Where it came from
NIST explicitly attributes this release to Executive Order 14306. This is a different authority link from FISMA’s original instruction to develop the catalog.
What changed—or stayed
The release does not amend FISMA or A-130. The checked change list does not revise SA-9; the discussion above quotes the preserved 2020 catalog. SP 800-53B’s release number changed for consistency, with no baseline changes in this release.

SP 800-18 Revision 2 puts data flows into system plans

2026NIST guidance

The June revision combines planning guidance for security, privacy, and cybersecurity supply-chain risk. It says plans should describe the data lifecycle, responsible people, components and flows, and risks from information shared outside the system.

Where it came from
Its executive summary explicitly connects the planning duty to A-130 and its planning activities to SP 800-37. Its authority statement cites FISMA 2014.
What changed—or stayed
The latest planning guide still treats information and systems together without making their boundaries identical. Nonfederal organizations may use this guide voluntarily; a separate legal, policy, or agreement requirement must be identified where its use is compulsory.

What agencies must do now

Start with the information and the service being acquired. Then identify every applicable duty. The texts do not support a blanket rule that commercial services are outside federal information-system obligations, or that only directly controlled components count.

They do support different requirements in specifically defined nonfederal settings. The clearest example here is CUI confidentiality under 32 CFR 2002.14(h)(2) and SP 800-171. That distinction must be applied with its scope and exceptions intact.

What must be protected?

Information duty
Information collected or maintained by or on behalf of the agency, with protections proportionate to harm.
System duty
Systems used or operated by the agency or by another organization on its behalf, under the same statutory risk standard.

What does the agency have to do?

Information duty
Assess harm, determine needed protection, implement risk-reducing policies, test controls, and carry applicable security and privacy duties into agreements.
System duty
Include covered systems in the agency program; categorize, select and assess controls, authorize, and monitor under the applicable standards and policy.

What if another organization handles the information?

Information duty
A-130 §4(j)(1) requires suitable terms in lifecycle agreements. Section 4(j)(2) also reaches systems that collect or maintain federal information on behalf of government.
System duty
The same §4(j)(2) covers systems used or operated on behalf of government. Its listed oversight duties include controls, inventory, access, interfaces, and incident response.

What changes for qualifying nonfederal CUI systems?

Information duty
The CUI rule directs confidentiality requirements through SP 800-171, with its exceptions. Requirements apply to relevant components, including components protecting the CUI.
System duty
The CUI rule treats systems operated for the agency as agency systems and allows the agency’s additional internal requirements. It explicitly includes outsourced processing for mixed users.

Who accepts the risk?

Information duty
The information owner or steward retains responsibility for appropriate use and protection when information is shared.
System duty
The authorizing official may authorize system operation, common controls, or use of an external system, service, or application.

Does a boundary settle the legal scope?

Information duty
No. Information can leave a system boundary while protection and agreement duties continue.
System duty
No. An external service may be outside one authorization boundary while still subject to applicable controls, assurance, and oversight.

A practical reading order for an agency decision

  1. Identify the information, its lifecycle, and any separate privacy, records, confidentiality, or CUI requirements.
  2. Determine what the other organization does: collects or maintains information for the agency, operates a system for it, receives information for another purpose, or exchanges information across a boundary. These can overlap.
  3. Apply the appropriate policy and standard to that arrangement. Do not substitute “commercial,” “shared,” or “outside our boundary” for the actual scope language.
  4. Put the necessary protections, oversight, access, interfaces, incident duties, and evidence requirements into suitable agreements. Categorize and authorize where required.
  5. Monitor performance and changing risk. Preserve the information owner’s responsibilities and the agency official’s decision record.

This reading order is this report’s synthesis of 44 USC 3554, A-130 §4(j), SP 800-37’s role descriptions, and SP 800-47’s exchange guidance. It is not an additional rule.
