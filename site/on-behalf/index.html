Federal information in private services

Buying a service, operating a system, and protecting information are different responsibilities. The wording tells us which duties travel with the information.

The distinction

An agency can remain responsible for information without operating the private system that handles it. Some requirements follow the information; others attach to a covered system, a particular service, or an agreement. The distinction is real. It is not a blanket exemption for commercially sold services.

This report separates three arrangements: an agency system; an external service collecting or maintaining information for the agency; and a qualifying nonfederal system receiving information for other work. Their obligations can overlap. “Private company,” “external service,” and “nonfederal system” are not interchangeable legal labels.

The question is what an agency must do when it buys access to a provider-run service without taking over its operation. Quotations below supply the rule; the following paragraphs explain its reach and limits.

FISMA: two objects of protection

Quoted passages appear in full below each heading, with their authority and location. Excerpts retain their wording; text extraction normalizes line breaks, line-end word breaks, and spacing before punctuation. Explanations are separate from the quotations.

FISMA assigns two duties under one protection standard

These are separate objects of the agency head’s duty, joined by “and.” The first is information collected or maintained by or on behalf of the agency. The second is systems used or operated by the specified parties. Neither limb says that the agency must own the equipment.

The information clause does not first require declaring a recipient’s entire system federal. Conversely, the system clause does not say only “operated”: it also says “used,” and expressly includes contractor and other-organization operation. This text alone does not resolve which part of a purchased service constitutes the covered system.

The excerpts are from the 2014 enactment. The collected current Code retains these two clauses. The phrases “created,” “processed,” and “disposed of” belong to the broader information lifecycle language in A-130, not this sentence of FISMA.

Risk management is a duty, not the only duty

Section 3554(a)(2) assigns officials risk assessment, selection of appropriate protection, risk reduction, and testing. Section 3554(b) requires an agency-wide program that includes outside support. The agency cannot satisfy all of this merely by acknowledging that a private service is risky.

These are obligations imposed on the agency. A provider’s particular obligations must be traced to an applicable law, regulation, contract, or other agreement. An agency’s accountability does not itself confer ownership of the provider or unlimited authority over its business.

Service purchase, system operation, and agency responsibility

Buying access is different from commissioning operation

A supplier may independently design, own, run, and sell a standard service. The agency buys access and controls its own users and content. A different contract may require a company to build and operate a specified agency application. Those arrangements distribute operational responsibility differently.

NIST’s system owner is a management role, not a statement about legal title to equipment. Its authorizing official may accept responsibility for operation OR for use of an external service. An agency authorization to use therefore need not mean that the agency operates the provider’s platform.

The presence of an authorizing official does not prove government operation. The absence of a government operator does not prove that a system falls outside FISMA. The statutory classification and the authorization arrangement must each be supported, rather than inferred from the other.

A federal decision maker does not become the platform operator

A-130 requires a senior Federal official to authorize operation. FIPS 200 imposes minimum-security requirements on agencies through control selection. These are concrete governance and safeguard requirements; a provider’s commercial status is not a substitute for analyzing their applicability.

The role of the federal official is to make the agency’s authorization decision. NIST’s external-service model separately recognizes provider operation and agency authorization to use. The cited provisions do not establish that a system must have a pre-existing government-appointed system owner before it can meet the statutory system clause. Failure to assign a required role is not itself an exemption.

For a purchased service, record the agency official who accepts the use risk, the provider accountable for service operation, the agency information owner, and the responsibilities the agreement actually grants. Do not describe an agency use authorization as operational control over a private company.

Information stewardship can remain with the agency

This is the clearest practical distinction: the agency can set the permitted purpose, recipients, retention, and protection needs for its information while a private company runs the service. Its information owner and the service’s system owner need not be the same person.

The agency can directly control accounts, sharing, exports, endpoint protection, and some tenant settings. For provider-managed functions, it may need agreed commitments, assurance evidence, technical safeguards, or limits on what information may enter the service. Those mechanisms allocate responsibility; they do not transfer operation of the whole platform.

The difficult boundary is the work being supplied

Direct agency control is relevant, but NIST does not make it an absolute condition of authorization. Its management concept includes budgetary and programmatic authority, and it recognizes external systems outside direct control.

The CUI rule goes further within its own scope: outsourced information-processing services can be systems operated on behalf of an agency, including systems with private customers. Selling a shared service and performing work on behalf of an agency are therefore not mutually exclusive under that rule.

This does not settle the government-wide definition for every use of ordinary information. DHS’s 2023 response expressly declined to adopt the CUI definition as the general FISMA definition. But it prevents treating “commercial service” as an established categorical exclusion. The report distinguishes this unresolved classification issue from the agency’s independently stated information duties.

What agencies must do for externally handled information

A-130 Appendix I §4(j) addresses non-Federal entities. Its first requirement concerns agreements involving federal information. Its second has two alternative triggers and six specific oversight duties. The quotations preserve that distinction.

1. Put the required protections into the arrangement

Actor: the agency. Trigger: contracts or other agreements involving the listed activities with federal information. This obligation reaches information handling; its text is not limited to a contract to operate an agency system.

The agency must identify its security and privacy requirements and obtain terms sufficient to meet them. A normal subscription can leave the vendor in operational control, but the agency still has to determine whether the arrangement permits lawful use. This clause does not grant an agency unlimited power to rewrite a supplier’s business practices.

2. Apply the separate information-handling trigger

The word “or” matters. OMB covers systems used or operated on behalf of government OR systems that collect or maintain federal information on its behalf. Calling the provider’s platform nonfederal does not, by itself, answer whether this second trigger applies.

The heading is “Non-Federal Entities.” That is a description of the counterparties, not a declaration that all of their systems are nonfederal. The following requirements attach to the systems described in this sentence. They should not be silently extended to unrelated corporate systems or every private recipient of public information.

3. Establish access oversight and verify protections

Actor: the agency, for systems meeting §4(j)(2). The duties include access and vetting oversight and ensuring that controls are effective and comply with the stated requirements. Practical evidence can come from the provider, assessments, and the agreed service arrangements; the agency need not operate every control itself.

This is stronger than a risk assessment alone. It also does not mean every private system receives an identical control set: the applicable standard, information, service, and agreement must be identified.

Additional triggers, express limits, and overlapping guidance

Ordinary federal information is the starting point. The provisions below add conditions for specific information or services; they do not all apply to every document.

CUI: an express limit on treating private systems as agency systems

These are binding regulatory words within the CUI program. They expressly distinguish the protection of information on a qualifying nonfederal system from treating that system as the agency’s own.

The same paragraph directs agencies to SP 800-171 for CUI confidentiality, unless the listed authority prescribes specific safeguards or an agreement establishes protection above moderate confidentiality for CUI Basic. The preceding paragraph covers outsourced processing, including mixed public/private customer systems. Both sides of the rule must be retained.

An entity receiving specifications to manufacture a product is different from an entity hired to process or host the agency’s records under this rule. “Incidental” describes the purpose of the receipt, not simply that the service is commercially available.

SP 800-171 is a bounded branch, not a substitute for every external service

Revision 3 states that its requirements do not apply to organizations collecting or maintaining information on behalf of an agency, as well as those using or operating a system on its behalf. Footnote 8 directs those activities to FISMA. The information limb therefore cannot simply be assigned wholesale to SP 800-171.

For the branch where SP 800-171 does apply, component scope and a separate security domain can limit implementation to CUI-handling and protecting components. The document focuses on CUI confidentiality; it is not a general exemption from other applicable laws or agreements.

The collected 2025 CFR incorporates the June 2015 publication with January 2016 updates. Revision 3 is current NIST guidance in this review, not proof that every contract or regulation automatically adopted it. Identify the governing edition for the actual arrangement.

M-24-15 uses information-handling verbs to set FedRAMP’s scope, subject to express exclusions. It does not limit that scope to services the government itself operates. Its footnote distinguishes agency authorization to use from authorization to operate.

This is why service purchase versus delegated operation matters without resolving everything. An external service can remain provider-run and still require a specified assessment or authorization for agency use. The relevant offering is not automatically the provider’s entire company.

Additional duties need their own trigger

Federal contract information: FAR 52.204-21 applies through the covered contract clause. Its definition excludes publicly released information and simple payment transactions. A company’s covered contractor system receives specified safeguards, not an automatic conversion of its entire business into an agency system.

Personal information: Privacy Act subsection (m) has a specific trigger—contracting for operation of a system of records to accomplish an agency function. Not every document with a person’s name is such a system; subsection (a)(5) concerns records retrieved by an individual’s name or other identifying particular. Other privacy obligations may apply even where (m) does not.

These provisions illustrate why an obligation matrix must name its authority and trigger. “Federal information” alone does not make every rule in every information regime applicable.

Compare the examples

The first three examples compare buying SaaS access, operating an IaaS workload, and commissioning application operation. Incidental handling and internet transit provide contrasting cases. These are stated assumptions, not vendor compliance findings.

Design work in Figma

Assumptions. Agency staff use a commercial design workspace for website layouts. The provider stores designs and comments. No claim is made about any actual Figma offering, contract, authorization, or security architecture.

The fact that it is commercial SaaS does not settle “on behalf of.”

The agency creates the substantive content; the service processes and maintains it. That can be information handling for the agency even though the agency does not operate the provider’s platform. In the CUI framework, a service whose job is information processing is different from receiving information merely incidentally to an unrelated service.

Simple website layouts may carry little confidentiality risk. That does not prove the service is out of scope: unreleased plans, research notes, user data, access credentials, or deployment integrations change the facts. A tenant can allocate user-management duties while provider controls remain relevant.

For an agency-approved public or nonsensitive collaboration use, the retrieved FedRAMP guidance supplies examples of exclusions that may be relevant. Applying one requires a use-case analysis; the report does not equate Figma with an expressly excluded product. Even an excluded use can leave security, privacy, records, and appropriate-use duties.

An agency application on AWS

Assumptions. The agency manages an application and virtual resources on commercial IaaS. AWS is an illustration of the service model, not a finding about any region, product, contract, or current authorization.

The agency workload and the infrastructure can have separate boundaries and connected responsibilities.

The agency can operate its application while the provider operates infrastructure that processes and stores agency information. The presence of an agency-controlled tenant does not mean the infrastructure performs no work on its behalf.

An application-only authorization may inherit protections from its host. The agency must identify which protections are inherited, what evidence supports them, and what it still implements. A host authorization does not authorize every application, configuration, integration, or use of information.

The provider’s service boundary can include shared components relevant to confidentiality, integrity, and availability without embracing every corporate function or other customer’s workload. The exact boundary follows the offering and its architecture.

Agency
Application code, workload configuration, permissions, data, application safeguards, and risk acceptance.
Infrastructure provider
The defined infrastructure service and its provider-implemented safeguards; details depend on the offering.
Boundary to examine
The application authorization, host-service authorization, inherited controls, and connections between them.
What could change the answer?

Adding a managed database, support access path, third-party backup service, or a different region can change the applicable service scope and evidence.

A contractor builds and runs the agency’s application

Assumptions. A contract expressly requires a company to build and operate an agency application on commercial cloud infrastructure. The application serves one agency and is not itself a reusable shared offering.

Subcontracting the infrastructure does not end the contractor’s work on behalf of the agency.

The operating contract is strong evidence of agency-purpose system operation. The application may be a federal system even though both its operator and its infrastructure provider are private. The agency must arrange responsibilities across both layers.

M-24-15 excludes certain single-agency systems from FedRAMP. That program exclusion can apply to the custom application without excluding the host cloud service. It also does not exempt the custom system from agency security authorization or other applicable duties.

A development-only contract has different facts: delivering code is not the same as operating a production system. The developer’s internal business tools need their own analysis of information, access, contractual requirements, and any incidental processing.

Agency
Mission, requirements, oversight, information duties, authorization, and applicable contracting terms.
Application contractor
Agreed development and operational responsibilities, application controls, incident duties, and host integration.
Cloud provider
Host-service protections and evidence; the contractor must account for what the application inherits.
What could change the answer?

Selling the same application as a multi-agency SaaS offering changes the FedRAMP scope question. Removing operation from the contract changes the system-role analysis.

A company uses agency information to deliver another service

Assumptions. A supplier receives government specifications to manufacture a product. Its internal system supports that work; the agency has not outsourced an information-processing service to it.

A nonfederal system can still carry enforceable information-protection duties.

The CUI rule explicitly distinguishes information received only incidentally to a service or product other than processing services. Under that rule, the supplier’s system is not made federal merely by holding the information.

If the specifications are CUI, the rule directs agencies to SP 800-171 for CUI confidentiality on nonfederal systems, with stated exceptions. The publication allows a defined security domain and covers components processing, storing, transmitting, or protecting CUI. It does not require protecting the entire business to an undifferentiated agency standard.

For covered federal contract information, FAR 52.204-21 can impose safeguards through a contract clause. Public information and simple payment transaction information are excluded from that clause’s definition of federal contract information. These are specific limits, not a blanket exemption for commercial businesses.

Agency
Identify the information and applicable requirements; use lawful contract or agreement terms.
Supplier
Meet the applicable contract and information-protection obligations on its covered systems.
Boundary to examine
The components handling or protecting covered information, including dependencies; an isolated domain can reduce scope.
What could change the answer?

A contract to host, index, or process the agency’s records changes the purpose: information processing is then the purchased service, not incidental support for manufacturing.

Agency information crosses the internet

Assumptions. Agency endpoints exchange information over public networks. No single operator runs “the internet,” and ordinary transit is distinguished from a separately purchased managed network or cloud service.

A network dependency does not make the entire internet one federal system.

An information system is a discrete set of resources organized for information activities. An authorization covers a defined system and its responsibilities. Public networks may lie outside that boundary while still being part of the environment and a source of risk.

The agency protects the exchange through appropriate endpoint controls, authentication, encryption, connection rules, and agreements where applicable. SP 800-47 focuses on protection before, during, and after an exchange; it does not require ownership of the channel.

Do not turn this into “telecommunications can never be on behalf of government.” OMB’s outsourcing examples include telecommunications and managed services. A purchased managed network, a carrier contract, a cloud endpoint, and an arbitrary transit network can present different relationships.

Agency
Protect endpoints and exchanges, assess availability and interception risks, and authorize use.
Relevant service providers
Meet obligations for the specific services they supply; examine the actual contracts and legal regimes.
Boundary to examine
Defined endpoints and services, external connections, and risk controls—not the internet as a single owned asset.
What could change the answer?

Hiring a provider to run a private agency network or an application gateway adds a specific operated service to the analysis.

How the distinction developed

This history follows duties and definitions across different authorities. A later document does not automatically repeal an earlier rule or settle its meaning.

OMB expressly distinguished agency and contractor duties

This 1993 explanation is strong evidence for distinguishing an agency’s obligations from a counterparty’s. It does not say private services cannot handle information on behalf of an agency. The later security, CUI, and cloud provisions must be read on their own terms.

  1. 1974Law

    Congress addresses outsourced records about people

    The Privacy Act required agencies contracting for operation of a system of records to cause its requirements to apply when the operation accomplishes an agency function. Congress supplied a specific contractual bridge. It did not simply make every private recipient of government information a federal agency.

    Information management extends beyond the agency’s machines

        Coverage, source dates, and open questions

        • This is a civilian, unclassified analysis. National security systems, classified information, DoD contracting, CMMC, sector-specific secrecy laws, and every agency supplement are not comprehensively analyzed.
        • The report distinguishes issuance, effective dates, source editions, and retrieval dates. It does not reconstruct every intervening amendment or establish the original intent of Congress from policy documents written later.
        • The law links use the site’s OLRC release 119-118, through September 30, 2026. FOIA and records-definition additions use the 2024 Code. The CUI text is the 2025 annual CFR. October 24 attempts to retrieve current OLRC and eCFR text failed. These limits prevent a claim that every legal change through October 24 has been verified.
        • NIST publication status and the official FedRAMP scope source were checked on October 24, 2026. The FedRAMP source is program guidance and must be read alongside M-24-15. Its examples do not amend FISMA. The current-law discussion states the result supported by the identified editions, with the currency limits above.
        • Selected public comments are preserved as comments. EPIC and RAND do not speak for the government. The complete 2015 A-130 comment docket and a provision-by-provision OMB response were not recovered in this research. No causal claim is made that a particular comment produced final wording.
        • Inforegs supplied discovery and exact passage retrieval. Its semantic backfill was incomplete, so searches used lexical retrieval; results were not exhaustive. Original documents, surrounding provisions, and official publication pages were checked separately. A missing search hit is not evidence that no rule existed.
        • Figma and AWS illustrate service models under stated assumptions. This report does not inspect their private agreements, actual system boundaries, or current certification status. It offers a source-based analysis, not an agency authorization or a vendor compliance verdict.

        What still needs an agency-specific answer

        The actual information, purpose of the service, applicable contract, agency policies, system architecture, provider access, and available assurance determine the result for a real purchase. Where authorities differ in scope, record the reason for the chosen interpretation instead of treating a convenient definition as universal.
