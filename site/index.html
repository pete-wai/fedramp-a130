How federal information-security policy developed

The history of OMB Circular A-130, the laws that shaped it, and the technical standards agencies use to protect information.

How laws, agency policy, and technical standards fit together

Federal information security grew out of two related needs: managing government information well and protecting the computers that hold it. Congress gave agencies duties in both areas. The Office of Management and Budget (OMB), part of the Executive Office of the President, turns many of those duties into government-wide policy. Its Circular A-130 brings together rules for managing information, buying technology, protecting privacy, and securing systems. A circular is a set of instructions OMB issues to federal agencies.

The National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST), within the Department of Commerce, develops the technical standards and guidance agencies use. Its predecessor, the National Bureau of Standards, was doing computer-security work before A-130 appeared in 1985. Congress has repeatedly assigned standards work directly to NIST. OMB and NIST also work together: OMB sets agency expectations, while NIST develops methods for meeting them. In some cases, NIST’s existing work helps shape OMB policy.

A-130’s central contribution is to make security part of everyday agency management. Officials must plan for it, fund it, assign responsibility, and decide whether a system’s risks are acceptable. Technical standards supply much of the detail. Federal Information Processing Standards (FIPS) establish standards for specified federal uses; NIST Special Publications (SP) explain methods and practices. A law, policy, standard, regulation, or contract determines when particular guidance is required.

Cloud services brought these responsibilities into a shared setting. Agencies increasingly relied on commercially operated systems, while remaining responsible for their information. The Federal Risk and Authorization Management Program (FedRAMP), established by OMB in 2011 and written into law in 2022, lets agencies reuse security assessments of cloud services. Its requirements combine A-130’s agency responsibilities with NIST standards and cloud-specific law and policy.1

Before A-130: computers, personal records, and security

In the 1960s, federal computer policy focused on acquiring equipment, sharing expensive machines, and deciding who would manage them. In March 1965, the Bureau of the Budget, OMB’s predecessor, issued Circular A-71 to divide those responsibilities among agencies, the General Services Administration, and the Department of Commerce. The Brooks Act followed in October. It gave Commerce a statutory role in computer standards and technical services.

As agencies collected more personal information, Congress added safeguards. The Privacy Act of 1974 required agencies to protect records about individuals and assigned OMB responsibility for implementation guidance. The National Bureau of Standards developed technical guidance on physical security and risk analysis, as well as the Data Encryption Standard. These publications gave agencies practical tools for protecting information and computer facilities.

A 1978 addition to A-71 brought security of automated systems into OMB’s management instructions. Agencies were expected to plan for risks and disruptions. The National Bureau of Standards’ 1982 guide to contingency planning helped managers carry out those responsibilities, including preparing to continue essential work when computers were unavailable.

The Paperwork Reduction Act of 1980 gave OMB a broader information-management role, covering how agencies collected, used, maintained, and shared information. A-130 brought these strands together in 1985: information policy, technology management, privacy, records, and security.23

How A-130 changed

Six major editions show how A-130 changed as federal computing, public services, and security responsibilities developed.

1985 · Bringing information policy together

OMB issued A-130 in December 1985 to bring government information policy into one place. Agencies had previously worked under several circulars covering computers, records, privacy, and coordination with state and local governments. The new circular replaced four of them and carried forward the security responsibilities introduced under A-71. It took effect on December 12 and appeared in the Federal Register, the government’s official publication of rules and notices, on December 24.

The Paperwork Reduction Act of 1980 provided the main framework. Congress had assigned OMB responsibility for government-wide information management and required agencies to manage their information resources. A-130 connected those duties with the Privacy Act’s protections for personal records and the Brooks Act’s provisions for computer equipment and standards. It addressed both the public value of government information and the resources needed to collect, maintain, and use it.

Security was part of that larger management task. Appendix III required agencies to establish security programs, assess risks, review protections, and prepare for disruptions. Much of the policy reflected a period when agencies depended on centralized computer facilities. Managers needed to protect those facilities and ensure that essential work could continue after an equipment failure or other emergency.

Technical standards were already available. The National Bureau of Standards had published guidance on physical security and risk analysis, along with an encryption standard. A-130 connected this technical work to agency planning and accountability. Its lasting contribution was to put responsibility for security alongside decisions about budgets, equipment, staff, and information—not to leave protection solely to computer specialists. Later revisions would adapt that approach as agencies moved from large central computers to interconnected systems and commercial services.

1993 · Making government information easier to use

The 1993 revision focused on how agencies collected, managed, and shared information. Electronic records and new ways of distributing information were changing the relationship between government and the public. OMB asked agencies to plan for information throughout its life: from collection and use to public access, preservation, and disposal. The revision took effect on June 25 and was published on July 2.

Agencies were responsible for making information available in ways that supported their missions. They also needed to consider the needs of people using that information and consult with state and local governments. OMB folded its separate policies on government publications and audiovisual activities into A-130, making the circular a more complete guide to public information. Its definition of government information included information handled for the government, extending the management perspective beyond an agency’s own offices.

Several laws had changed since 1985. Amendments to the Paperwork Reduction Act affected information management. The Computer Security Act assigned security-planning and training duties to agencies and standards work to the National Bureau of Standards, renamed NIST in 1988. The Computer Matching and Privacy Protection Act and its amendments added protections when agencies compared personal records across systems. The revised circular also cited the Chief Financial Officers Act.

OMB updated its privacy appendix to reflect the matching requirements but left Appendix III, the security appendix, unchanged. Further work on technology management and security was still to come. Meanwhile, NIST continued developing security standards under its statutory responsibilities. The 1993 update therefore matters chiefly for its treatment of information as something agencies must manage from beginning to end, including the public’s ability to find and use it. That broader view would remain important as later revisions brought security more closely into agency planning.

1994 · Connecting technology spending to public services

The 1994 revision asked agencies to judge technology by the work it helped them accomplish. OMB wanted better public services, lower administrative costs, and less burden on the public. Agencies needed to consider whether an investment would improve their operations before committing money to a system. The revision took effect on July 15 and was published on July 25.

This approach broadened the management of computers into the management of information and services. Agencies were expected to connect technology choices with their missions, examine work processes, and plan investments accordingly. A faster computer was useful only if the resulting system helped the agency do its job. Section 8b, which addressed information systems and technology, became an important part of that connection between spending and performance.

The Paperwork Reduction Act supplied OMB’s information-management responsibilities. The Computer Security Act remained part of the security framework, while the Government Performance and Results Act reflected a wider emphasis on agency goals and results. Together, this legal setting encouraged agencies to think about the purpose and performance of their systems as well as their equipment. Congress would strengthen technology-investment responsibilities further in the 1995 Paperwork Reduction Act and the 1996 Clinger–Cohen legislation.

NIST’s technical standards program continued alongside these management changes. FIPS 140-1, approved in January 1994, set security requirements for cryptographic modules—the hardware or software components that perform encryption and related functions. Its authority came from the federal standards and computer-security laws. OMB’s revision addressed the agency decisions surrounding technology; NIST and Commerce supplied standards for particular technical uses. The next major A-130 revision, in 1996, would turn directly to security management and replace the older security appendix.

1996 · Making managers responsible for security risk

By 1996, federal computing had spread beyond central facilities into networks, desktop systems, and connected applications. OMB rewrote A-130’s security appendix for that environment. The revision, dated February 8 and published on February 20, placed responsibility on managers to understand their systems’ risks and provide appropriate protection.

The Computer Security Act and the Paperwork Reduction Act of 1995 were central to the update. OMB incorporated the security-planning instructions it had issued in Bulletins 88-16 and 90-08. Agencies still needed security plans, staff training, contingency arrangements, and reviews, but they no longer had to follow the same centralized process for submitting plans. The appendix distinguished shared computing environments, called general support systems, from major applications with their own particular risks.

Managers also had to authorize systems to operate. This meant accepting responsibility for the risks of using a system after considering its protections. Security plans and reviews supported that decision. OMB retained the duty to assess risk while allowing more flexibility in how agencies did so; a formal numerical risk analysis was no longer required for every system. Rules of behavior made employees’ responsibilities part of the security program.

NIST’s work helped shape this approach. OMB drew on the computer-security handbook’s discussion of policies for individual systems. NIST’s 1998 security-planning guide, SP 800-18, then gave agencies practical help with the circular’s planning requirements. Technical guidance and management policy developed together.

Clinger–Cohen became law on February 10, between the revision’s date and its publication. OMB’s accompanying explanation anticipated further technology-management guidance. The February revision’s main change was already clear: agency managers were responsible for choosing safeguards, preparing staff, reviewing security, and accepting the risks of operating their systems.

2000 · Building security into technology investment

The 2000 revision brought A-130 into line with the technology-management reforms of the late 1990s. Clinger–Cohen had strengthened agency responsibility for choosing and overseeing investments and established a larger role for chief information officers. OMB’s November 28 update connected those responsibilities with the circular’s existing policies for information and security. The final notice appeared on December 12.

Agencies were expected to plan their technology investments, compare choices, and monitor results. They also needed an enterprise architecture: a description of how their information, systems, and business processes fit together. These requirements aimed to make individual purchases serve the agency’s wider mission. Chief information officers helped connect technical decisions with budgets and management priorities.

Security became an explicit part of investment decisions. Earlier that year, memorandum M-00-07 had instructed agencies to include security planning and costs in information-system investments. OMB incorporated that guidance into A-130. Agencies therefore needed to account for protection when planning and funding a system, including the continuing costs of operating it securely. This gave security a place in the same decisions that determined a system’s purpose, design, and resources.

The Government Paperwork Elimination Act of 1998 added another task: expanding electronic transactions. OMB incorporated its implementation guidance as Appendix II, covering electronic records and signatures. Agencies had to consider the risks of a transaction when deciding how to handle it electronically.

The wider security framework continued to change while this edition remained in use. The Government Information Security Reform Act of 2000 introduced security-program and reporting duties. The Federal Information Security Management Act of 2002, known as FISMA, then assigned NIST work on risk categories and minimum security requirements. Agencies used the resulting standards alongside the 2000 circular until OMB comprehensively rewrote A-130 in 2016.

2016 · Managing security and privacy throughout a system’s life

OMB comprehensively rewrote A-130 in July 2016 after sixteen years of changes in law, technology, and government services. Agencies were using interconnected systems and commercial services to deliver more of their work. The new circular brought security and privacy into decisions throughout a system’s life, from planning and purchasing to operation and retirement.

The revised title, Managing Information as a Strategic Resource, reflected this broader purpose. Information supported public services, accountability, and agency performance. Protecting it required coordination among leaders, technology staff, privacy officials, purchasing teams, and the people responsible for agency programs. Security and privacy needed attention while systems were being designed and acquired, as well as after they began operating.

The legal framework included the Paperwork Reduction Act, Clinger–Cohen, the E-Government Act, and FISMA. The 2014 Federal Information Security Modernization Act had updated the division of security responsibilities among OMB, the Department of Homeland Security, and agencies. The circular connected those responsibilities with NIST’s established standards and guidance for assessing risks, selecting safeguards, authorizing systems, and monitoring their operation.

OMB also reorganized the appendices. Appendix I covered protection and management of federal information resources; Appendix II covered personally identifiable information and agency privacy programs. Privacy received attention throughout the information life cycle, including the risks arising from how agencies collected and used information. Agency responsibilities extended to information handled on their behalf, and the circular expressly addressed cloud-based services.

NIST later updated its Risk Management Framework in SP 800-37 Revision 2 to integrate security and privacy more closely. OMB continued issuing memoranda on particular subjects, including identity, cloud services, software security, and zero trust—a security approach that checks access rather than assuming a user or device is trusted because it is inside a network.

Who is responsible for each part of security?

Responsibility varies by subject. A-130 provides much of the management framework, while laws and presidential directives also assign work directly to NIST and other agencies.

Agency planning and accountability

OMB connects security with agency management.

A-130 brings security into budgets, investment decisions, staff responsibilities, and the planning and operation of systems. The Paperwork Reduction Act and FISMA give OMB and agency heads the underlying duties. NIST supplies technical methods that help agencies carry them out.19

Security categories and minimum safeguards

Congress assigned the technical work to NIST.

FISMA 2002 directed NIST to develop categories based on the harm a security failure could cause, along with minimum security requirements. FIPS 199 and FIPS 200 put those assignments into standards. Agencies use them with SP 800-53 to choose safeguards; A-130 incorporates their use into agency management.20

Deciding whether a system may operate

Agency officials accept risk; NIST provides a method.

The 1996 circular required management authorization and security reviews. NIST’s SP 800-37 later developed the Risk Management Framework, a process for selecting, assessing, and monitoring safeguards. The agency official responsible for authorization decides whether the remaining risk is acceptable.21

Encryption

NIST develops standards; OMB directs agency adoption.

Federal encryption standards began before A-130. NIST and Commerce continued that work under computer-security and standards legislation. More recently, the Quantum Computing Cybersecurity Preparedness Act assigned OMB migration duties linked to NIST standards designed to resist attacks using quantum computers.22

Identity and access

Presidential directives and OMB memoranda set specific tasks.

A 2004 presidential directive required a common identification standard for federal employees and contractors, leading to FIPS 201. Separately, OMB’s M-04-04 directed NIST to develop guidance for electronic authentication—checking that someone accessing an online service is who they claim to be.23

Contractors handling sensitive information

Requirements depend on the work and the agreement.

A-130 covers federal information handled on an agency’s behalf. A separate program governs controlled unclassified information, or CUI: information that needs safeguards under law or policy but is not classified. NIST’s SP 800-171 provides requirements for protecting CUI in nonfederal systems; applicable regulations and agreements determine a contractor’s obligations.24

Cloud services and FedRAMP

A shared assessment program supports agency decisions.

The 2011 FedRAMP memorandum established a common approach to assessing cloud services. Congress put the program into law in 2022, using A-130’s authorization terminology and NIST’s definition of cloud computing. OMB’s M-24-15 updated the program’s scope and responsibilities in 2024 and replaced the 2011 memorandum.25

National-security systems

A separate set of rules applies.

A-130’s 2016 scope provision excludes national-security systems from mandatory application of the circular. Agencies follow the statutes, executive orders, directives, and agency policies applicable to those systems. FISMA also gives these systems separate treatment.26

When technical guidance becomes a requirement

A NIST publication becomes a requirement for a particular use through the authority that adopts it. That may be a federal standard, an OMB policy, a regulation, or a contract. Agency officials remain responsible for carrying out the duties assigned to them by law and policy. This is why the technical standard and the agency instruction often need to be read together.27

How the technical standards developed

Early guidance: protecting facilities and keeping services running

Before A-130, the National Bureau of Standards published guidance on physical security and risk analysis in FIPS 31 and FIPS 65. Its 1982 contingency-planning guide helped managers prepare for computer disruptions and meet the responsibilities introduced under A-71. The Data Encryption Standard supplied a separate technical tool for protecting information.28

Turning security policy into practical plans

NIST’s computer-security handbook, SP 800-12, explained management and technical practices. OMB drew on it when revising the security appendix in 1996. SP 800-18 then explained how to write the security plans A-130 required. SP 800-30 developed guidance for assessing risk, helping agencies identify threats and choose protections.29

From the consequences of a failure to the choice of safeguards

FISMA gave NIST a sequence of technical tasks. FIPS 199, issued in 2004, classifies information and systems by the potential harm from a security failure. FIPS 200, issued in 2006, sets minimum requirements and directs agencies to choose controls—specific safeguards—from SP 800-53. The control catalog has expanded over time: Revision 4 added a privacy appendix, while Revision 5 integrated security and privacy controls. SP 800-53B supplies the baseline sets from which agencies begin selecting controls.30

Assessing risk throughout a system’s life

The first SP 800-37, published in 2004, described certification and accreditation: evaluating safeguards and approving a system’s operation. The 2010 revision placed those activities within a Risk Management Framework covering the system’s life cycle. The 2018 revision added organizational preparation and integrated privacy responsibilities. SP 800-137 supports the continuing monitoring needed to understand how risks and safeguards change after a system begins operating.31

Encryption and the move toward quantum-resistant standards

The 1977 Data Encryption Standard was an early federal encryption standard. Later publications addressed both encryption methods, such as the Advanced Encryption Standard in FIPS 197, and the security of cryptographic hardware and software through the FIPS 140 series. In 2024, NIST published FIPS 203, 204, and 205 for cryptography designed to withstand quantum-computing attacks. The 2022 quantum-preparedness law linked OMB’s migration instructions to NIST standards; M-26-15 set further migration policy in June 2026.32

Proving identity in person and online

Homeland Security Presidential Directive 12, issued in August 2004, called for a common identification standard for federal employees and contractors. FIPS 201 followed in 2005. OMB’s M-04-04 addressed a different need: assessing the identity checks required for electronic services. It directed NIST to develop technical guidance, which became SP 800-63. Later editions updated digital-identity practices, while M-19-17 revised agency identity and access policy in 2019.3334

Cloud services, contractor systems, and newer security practices

NIST’s 2011 cloud publications defined cloud computing and explained the security and privacy issues agencies faced when using public cloud services. SP 800-171 later addressed sensitive unclassified information in nonfederal systems. SP 800-207 described zero-trust architecture in 2020; subsequent presidential and OMB policy directed agencies to adopt that approach. SP 800-218 supplied a framework for secure software development, supporting the software-security work ordered in Executive Order 14028. OMB memoranda M-22-18 and M-23-16 applied that work to federal software purchases.35

How OMB policy changed between revisions

OMB updates agency instructions between major revisions of A-130 through memoranda. Some respond to new laws; others address a particular technology or management problem. Annual instructions tell agencies how to report on their security and privacy programs. Together, these documents explain how the broad responsibilities in the circular become specific agency tasks.

Some memoranda later became part of A-130 itself. M-95-22 explained the 1995 Paperwork Reduction Act’s public-information provisions, and OMB incorporated it into the 1996 explanatory appendix. M-00-07 required agencies to plan and fund security as part of technology investments; the 2000 revision incorporated that instruction. Other memoranda assigned technical work directly, as M-04-04 did when it asked NIST to develop electronic-authentication guidance.

Cloud policy followed a similar pattern. OMB established FedRAMP through a memorandum in December 2011. Congress enacted the FedRAMP Authorization Act in 2022, and OMB issued M-24-15 in 2024 to update the program. That memorandum replaced the 2011 instructions.

Policies also change through withdrawal or replacement. M-19-17 replaced several earlier identity instructions. M-17-26 withdrew some policies while modifying or pausing others. The library identifies these changes where the successor document records them.36
